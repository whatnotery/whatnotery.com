Review : Traumatized by Catherine Liu

The New Politics of Public Suffering

Nov 07, 2026

In Catherine Liu’s new book, Traumatized: The New Politics of Public Suffering, she explores the idea that the personalization of suffering and trauma under neoliberalism is based largely on the erasure of the genuine traumas from both the visceral and unspoken violence inflicted on the working class by global capital. The book is a quick read at under 200 pages and is divided into four chapters, along with an introduction and conclusion. The first chapter, “Innocence and Trauma: Liberalism, Class Formation, and Modern Childhood,” explores the dynamics of how childhood has changed pre- and post-industrial revolution, as well as how those dynamics were divided between the classes. It also explores how modern child welfare came from a culture of upper-middle-class “activism” that addressed the evils of dangerous child labor as an individual failing on the part of the child’s family, as opposed to a systemic problem that the capitalist class encourages. Most of the book is an exploration of how the evils of systemic capitalism are broken down into individual cases without wider material analysis, and the second chapter, “The Political Gets Personal: Trauma Culture and PMC Feminism,” is no exception. The chapter explores how second-wave feminists distanced themselves from the labor feminism that came before them and cast women’s issues in the context of the individual struggles a woman might face, and more specifically, what issues a white middle-class woman might face. The third chapter primarily explores the development of PTSD as a diagnosis and the political malleability of psychiatry and psychology as a “science,” particularly citing the political forces that shaped the development of the DSM diagnostic manual. I think this chapter has some weaknesses, in that the author reveals her preference for Freudian psychoanalysis and some of Freud’s less scientifically based theories. The final chapter is largely a treatise against Oprah Winfrey, who I think is to some extent a stand-in for neoliberal media’s focus on individual trauma overall. The conclusion, I think, makes some of the strongest arguments of the entire text, tying together the threads left by the prior chapters as a call to action against the neoliberal fetishization of trauma and survivorship in favor of a politics grounded in materialism and objective conditions.
